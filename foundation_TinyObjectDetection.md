### 1. Anchor design and the scale-mismatch trap
*"Your detector was trained with default anchor scales (e.g., 32px–512px, tuned for COCO-style objects), but your production use case is aerial drone footage where target objects (vehicles, people) are consistently 8–20px. You observe the model almost never fires on these objects even though similar objects are visible by eye. Walk through exactly why default anchors cause this failure at the IoU-matching stage during training (not just at inference), how you'd redesign the anchor configuration (scales, aspect ratios, strides) from your dataset's actual object-size distribution, and how you'd validate the new anchors are correctly matched before spending a full training cycle to find out."*
- Probes: understanding that anchor-to-GT IoU matching during training silently discards tiny objects as "unmatched" if no anchor is close enough in scale/position, ability to derive anchor configs empirically (e.g., k-means clustering on GT box sizes, as YOLO does) rather than guessing, and whether they validate matching statistics (recall of anchors against GT) before full training.

#### Detailed Answer — Anchor Design and the Scale-Mismatch Trap

##### Why default anchors fail at the IoU-matching stage during training (not just inference)

- Anchor-based detectors (Faster R-CNN, RetinaNet, YOLO-anchor variants) don't learn "detect small objects" directly — they learn to **refine a pre-defined anchor into the GT box**, and **which anchor owns which GT is fixed before training starts** by an IoU rule (e.g., RetinaNet: ≥ 0.5 positive, < 0.4 negative, in-between ignored; RPN: 0.7 / 0.3), sometimes with a low-quality-match fallback to the single best anchor.
- With default **32px–512px** anchors and **8–20px** objects, even a perfectly centred 32px anchor gives IoU ≈ area ratio: 8px → 0.06, 15px → $(15/32)^2 \approx 0.22$, 20px → 0.39. All below any standard matching threshold.
- **The consequence is worse than "no signal"**: these objects get no positives (so no classification/regression gradient), *and* the anchors sitting on them (IoU < 0.4) are labelled **background**, so the network is actively trained to suppress them. The fallback gives one weak positive (IoU ≈ 0.2) — noisy regression, outnumbered by negatives under focal loss.
- This explains why the model "almost never fires": it is a **target-assignment failure, not a capacity failure**. A stronger backbone can't fix it, and inference-time silence is just the downstream symptom.
- **Two compounding mismatches:**
  - *Grid alignment*: anchor centres sit on a stride-spaced grid, so a GT centre can be up to stride/2 off in x and y. Even a correctly sized 16px anchor on a 15px object, worst-case diagonal offset, gives IoU ≈ 0.38 at stride 8 (fails 0.5) but ≈ 0.61 at stride 4 — position-dependent, "random-looking" misses.
  - *Feature level*: a stride-32 cell summarises 32×32 pixels, so a 15px object is blended with background regardless of anchor size. Tiny objects need $P_2$/$P_3$ (stride 4/8).

##### Redesigning the anchor configuration from the dataset's actual size distribution

1. **k-means on GT (w, h) with a 1−IoU distance** (as in YOLOv2), not Euclidean — Euclidean is dominated by large boxes and leaves the tiny cluster one centroid. Use cluster medians, and compute sizes in **network-input pixels** after resize/letterbox (a 20px object in a 4K frame resized to 640 is ~3px).
2. **Cluster per size band/level** so the tiny band isn't starved of centroids, and choose k by anchor recall, not habit (more anchors at $P_2$ cost compute: a 1280² input has ~102k cells × 3 anchors).
3. **Align anchor size with level stride** (anchor ≈ 4–8× stride; torchvision FPN uses 8×), e.g. 8/16/32/64/128 on $P_2$–$P_6$ with a stride-4 $P_2$ for tiny objects. Keep larger anchors if medium/large objects can appear in production.
4. **Aspect ratios from the clusters** — top-down aerial vehicles/people are closer to 1:1–1:1.5 than COCO pedestrians' 1:2–1:3.
5. **Beyond hand-designed anchors (each with a trade-off):**
   - lower positive IoU threshold on small levels — more borderline positives, so precision can drop;
   - dynamic assignment (ATSS adaptive threshold, SimOTA/TAL) — more training cost and run-to-run variance;
   - anchor-free heads (FCOS, CenterNet, YOLOv8) — still have centre-sampling/scale rules, and very small boxes can get few positives;
   - NWD (Gaussian-distance) matching — smooth under 1–2px shifts, but not built into most frameworks and changes the training/eval metric.

##### Validating the new anchors before a full training cycle

Validate the **matching statistics** offline, in minutes, not after a multi-hour run:

1. **Best-IoU per GT**: for every GT box, the max IoU over all anchors at all levels/locations; plot the histogram.
2. **Anchor recall** = fraction of GT with best-IoU ≥ the positive threshold — a hard gate (e.g., > 95%), **stratified by object-size bucket**, because the overall number is dominated by large objects and hides tiny-object failure (same trap as Q3).
3. **Misalignment-aware check**: repeat with the GT shifted by stride/2 in x and y (worst case). A perfectly-centred check is optimistic and misses the grid-alignment problem.
4. **Positives per GT by size**: if tiny objects average ~1 positive and large ones ~9, tiny objects stay under-trained even when recall passes.
5. **Visual overlay** of matched anchors on real images — catches coordinate/resize-space bugs the numbers can hide.
6. Only after these pass, train — then confirm with AP_small and per-bucket recall.

### 2. Non-Maximum Suppression (NMS) collapsing dense small-object clusters
*"In a crowd-counting or warehouse pallet-counting application, your detector correctly localizes individual tiny objects in dense clusters during qualitative review, but production recall is much lower than expected specifically in high-density regions. Diagnose how standard NMS's IoU threshold is likely merging multiple true-positive detections into one surviving box, why this specific failure mode disproportionately affects small, closely-packed objects versus large well-separated ones, and what concrete alternative (Soft-NMS, DIoU-NMS, class-agnostic vs. per-class NMS, learned NMS/set-prediction heads like DETR) you'd evaluate — including the trade-off each introduces."*
- Probes: understanding that standard greedy NMS assumes "high overlap = duplicate," which breaks down when real adjacent tiny objects legitimately have high mutual IoU due to their small size and dense packing, concrete familiarity with NMS alternatives and their specific fix mechanisms, and awareness that "fixing" NMS can increase false positives — a trade-off that must be measured, not assumed.

#### Detailed Answer — NMS Collapsing Dense Small-Object Clusters

##### First, prove it is NMS (diagnosis before diagnosis)
Compare **pre-NMS vs post-NMS recall, stratified by local GT density** (neighbours within a radius). This separates three different failures that all look like "low recall in dense regions":
- **Pre-NMS recall already low in dense buckets** → upstream problem: two objects sharing one feature cell (one prediction for two objects), label assignment (see Q1), or the score threshold. NMS changes won't help.
- **Pre-NMS fine, post-NMS drops** → NMS-induced misses. This matches the symptom in the question: boxes look correct in qualitative review but vanish in the final output.
- **Pipeline caps that behave like NMS** → `max_det` (100/300 defaults), pre-NMS top-k per level, score cutoff. A 500-person crowd cannot be counted with a 100-box cap.

##### Why greedy NMS merges true positives
- NMS keeps the top-scoring box and **hard-deletes** every box with IoU > T. The built-in assumption is "overlap ⇒ duplicate".
- In a dense scene, real neighbours legitimately overlap. The lower-scoring true positive is deleted **regardless of its own confidence**, and the loss is **irreversible**: no score threshold can recover a box that no longer exists.

##### Why this disproportionately hits small, closely-packed objects
IoU itself is scale-invariant (for equal boxes it depends only on spacing-to-size, $(s-d)/(s+d)$), so "small" alone is not the reason. The real drivers:
1. **Packing density.** Distant crowds and stacked pallets have a small spacing-to-size ratio, so true-neighbour IoU sits around 0.3–0.6, straddling a typical T = 0.5.
2. **Localization jitter is large relative to box size (the key point).** A 3px jitter on a 10px box gives duplicate IoU ≈ 0.5, versus ≈ 0.94 on a 100px box. So for tiny objects the **duplicate-IoU range and the neighbour-IoU range overlap and no single T separates them**: low T kills real neighbours (recall loss), high T keeps duplicates (false positives, over-counting). For large objects the two ranges are far apart, so the problem doesn't appear.
3. **Stride quantisation and over-sized regression boxes** (extra context pixels) push neighbour overlap up further.

##### Alternatives and the trade-off each introduces
- **Tune T, and per-class vs class-agnostic NMS.** Cheap, but a higher T means more duplicates and over-counting. Per-class is required when different classes physically overlap (pallet + boxes on it) but allows the same object to survive with two labels; class-agnostic does the reverse. For single-class crowd counting the choice is irrelevant.
- **Soft-NMS** (decay the score instead of deleting; linear or Gaussian). Keeps real neighbours alive, but duplicates also survive unless a final score cutoff removes them, it shifts the score distribution (re-calibrate), it is $O(n^2)$, and it is poorly supported in deployment runtimes (fused hard-NMS plugins such as TensorRT's), so it may fall back to CPU.
- **DIoU-NMS** (IoU minus a centre-distance penalty). Free at inference and no retraining. It helps when neighbouring centres are separated; it does little for heavily overlapping or occluded objects with close centres, and it inherits the same centre jitter on tiny boxes.
- **Density-aware NMS (Adaptive-NMS) and crowd heads (CrowdDet).** These attack the real root cause, the single global threshold: the model predicts local density and raises T where it is high, or predicts several instances per proposal (set-NMS, EMD loss). The cost is retraining, extra heads and noisy density labels at small scale.
- **NMS-free set prediction (DETR, Deformable DETR, one-to-one-assignment YOLO variants).** Removes the threshold entirely, but historically weaker $AP_S$, slow convergence, **a fixed query count that is a hard cap on countable objects**, and ambiguous matching for near-identical dense objects.
- **Change the formulation.** If the real deliverable is a count, density-map or point-based counting avoids NMS altogether, at the cost of losing per-object boxes.

##### How I'd decide and how I'd prove the fix
- **Order of attack:** remove caps and the score-threshold issue → tune T / DIoU-NMS (no retraining) → Soft-NMS with a calibrated final cutoff → density-aware or NMS-free heads, or density-map counting.
- **Evidence:** per-density-bucket recall **and duplicate rate (boxes per GT)** across a sweep of T. If the curves cross, that confirms the overlapping-distribution problem. Report AR at a high max-detections setting, MR⁻² (CrowdHuman-style), and count MAE per density bucket, always with precision or duplicate rate beside recall so a "fix" is not just trading recall for false positives.
- **Latency check on target hardware:** dense scenes produce thousands of candidate boxes, so NMS cost and runtime support decide which option is deployable.

### 3. Why standard mAP hides catastrophic small-object failure
*"Your model reports a healthy overall mAP@0.5 of 0.82 on your validation set, and leadership is satisfied, but a safety/quality audit later reveals it misses the majority of a specific tiny-object class (e.g., small cracks in a pipeline inspection system) that matters most. Explain precisely why overall mAP can look strong while this specific failure goes undetected, what evaluation protocol (COCO's AP_small/medium/large, per-class AP, confusion analysis by object-size bucket) you would have mandated from day one, and how you'd design a size- and class-stratified reporting dashboard so this kind of blind spot can't silently reach production again."*
- Probes: deep understanding that mAP is an average across classes and often dominated by easy, large, frequent classes/objects, hands-on familiarity with COCO's official small/medium/large AP breakdown, and the organizational discipline to build monitoring that surfaces the metric that actually matters rather than the one that's easiest to report.

#### Detailed Answer — Why Standard mAP Hides Small-Object Failure

##### Why 0.82 mAP can coexist with a failing critical class
- **Class averaging.** mAP is a mean over classes. Nine classes at 0.90 plus a crack class at 0.18 still gives about 0.83, so one critical class moves the headline by only ~0.07, which looks like noise.
- **Instance pooling inside a class.** AP ranks all detections together, so large, confident, frequent objects fill the top of the ranking and dominate the curve. Tiny misses sit in the tail where they barely change the area.
- **Equal weighting ignores cost.** mAP has no notion of criticality. A missed pipeline crack and a missed minor label count the same.
- **AP integrates over all score thresholds; production runs at one.** Recall *at the deployed threshold* can be poor while AP still looks acceptable.
- **Lenient IoU and thin support.** AP@0.5 is forgiving, and a rare class with few validation instances gives a noisy AP with a wide confidence interval.
- **Thin defects break area-based size buckets.** A crack has small *area* but large *length*, so it can land in the wrong COCO bucket, and IoU on a thin box is hypersensitive to 1–2px shifts. Mask-based or length-based metrics are more honest here.

##### Evaluation protocol I would have mandated from day one
- **Per-class AP and recall at the operating threshold**, always reported next to mAP, never replaced by it.
- **Size-stratified metrics:** COCO AP_small / AP_medium / AP_large, plus domain-unit buckets (crack length or width in mm) and AP@[0.5:0.95] to expose localisation quality.
- **Operating-point metrics:** per-class PR curves, miss rate at a fixed false-positive rate (FROC / FPPI), so the number reflects a real deployment point.
- **Error decomposition** (TIDE-style: missed vs mislocalised vs misclassified vs background FP), sliced by size, location, lighting and camera, to turn a bad number into a cause.
- **Support counts and bootstrap confidence intervals** in every cell, with a minimum-support rule.
- **Synthetic stress test:** insert known-size defects into held-out backgrounds to get a clean size-vs-recall curve without dataset confounds.

##### Size- and class-stratified dashboard design
- **Class × size-bucket matrix**, each cell showing AP, recall@threshold, number of GT instances and CI, colour-coded against per-class targets.
- **Release gate on the critical class:** the *lower confidence bound* of recall in the smallest bucket must clear a threshold. An mAP improvement can never override it.
- **Release-over-release trend of the worst slice**, not just the average, plus condition slices (camera, shift, lighting) and a gallery of missed instances for human review.

##### Trade-offs
- More slices means more noise and multiple-comparison risk, so keep slices tied to business risk, enforce minimum support, and confirm on a held-out test set so per-class thresholds don't overfit validation.
- It supplements mAP rather than replacing it. The extra evaluation cost is small compared with a missed-defect incident reaching production.

### 4. Tiling/patch inference vs. single-pass inference trade-off at scale
*"You're deploying a tiny-object detector on 4K/8K satellite or industrial inspection imagery. Running the full image through the network at native resolution destroys small objects due to downsampling, but tiling the image into patches and running inference per-tile multiplies compute cost and introduces objects being split across tile boundaries. Walk through how you'd design the tiling strategy (patch size, overlap/stride, boundary-object stitching/merging logic) to balance throughput against accuracy, and how you would detect and correct duplicate or fragmented detections caused by objects straddling tile seams in production, not just in a one-off test."*
- Probes: practical experience with tiled inference pipelines (a very common real-world necessity for tiny-object detection on huge images), specific knowledge of overlap-and-merge strategies (e.g., overlapping tiles + NMS/box-merging across tile boundaries), and whether they have a systematic test (not ad hoc) for boundary-seam artifacts.

#### Interview Answer — Tiling Strategy and Boundary Stitching

**Interviewer:** Walk me through how you'd design the tiling strategy for running a tiny-object detector on 4K/8K satellite or industrial imagery, and how you'd detect and correct duplicate or fragmented detections from objects straddling tile seams — in production, not just a one-off test.

**Candidate:**

I'd split this into three parts: sizing the tiles, handling the seams, and keeping it correct once it's live.

**Why tiling is necessary at all.** An 8K image (7680×4320) resized to a typical 640px network input shrinks roughly 12×; a 15px object becomes ~1.2px, which is below what even a stride-4 feature map can represent. So native-resolution tiling is non-negotiable for tiny objects — but it trades that accuracy problem for three new ones: N× compute, objects physically cut by tile boundaries, and genuine duplicate detections wherever tiles overlap.

**Patch size.** I wouldn't pick this arbitrarily — I'd derive it from the dataset's actual object-size distribution, the same percentile/clustering analysis I'd use for anchor design, and size tiles so the largest relevant object sits comfortably inside a tile's core (roughly 3–4× its extent). The tile resolution should match the network's native trained input — resizing a tile down again just recreates the downsampling problem tiling was meant to solve. For throughput, I wouldn't reason from FLOPs alone: smaller tiles mean more tiles, and fixed per-call overhead (kernel launch, batching inefficiency) dominates at small sizes, so I'd actually benchmark images/sec on target hardware across candidate sizes. If compute is tight, I'd also consider adaptive tiling — a cheap coarse pass (saliency or density map) to tile finely only where objects are likely, skipping empty background like sky or warehouse floor.

**Overlap/stride.** Overlap needs to be at least as large as the biggest expected object plus a margin for localization jitter, so anything clipped by one tile boundary is still fully contained, uncut, in at least one neighboring tile — again derived from the real size distribution, not a default 10%. Too little overlap guarantees fragmentation; too much wastes compute and multiplies the number of duplicate detections I then have to reconcile, since an object near a seam can now appear in 2–4 tiles.

**Boundary stitching is really two distinct problems, and that distinction matters.** First, true duplicates — an object fully and independently detected in multiple overlapping tiles. Once I translate every detection into global image coordinates using each tile's known offset, that's a standard global NMS/Soft-NMS problem, identical to single-pass inference. Second, fragments — an object truncated by a tile's actual edge, or sitting almost symmetrically on the seam so neither tile sees it cleanly. Plain IoU-NMS doesn't reliably catch this case, because the two partial boxes can have low mutual IoU despite being the same object. For that I'd use a core-region/centroid-ownership convention: each tile's non-overlap "core" region owns any detection centered inside it, and only detections centered in the overlap band get compared against the neighboring tile for deduplication. That makes every pixel owned by exactly one tile, so merging is deterministic instead of relying on an IoU threshold to guess. Where neither tile saw the object cleanly, I'd fall back to confidence-weighted box averaging rather than a hard keep-best, since keep-best throws away real geometric information the other partial view actually had.

**Production monitoring is where most pipelines fall short**, because stitching usually gets validated once on a handful of test images and never checked again. I'd instrument every inference run to log the fraction of final detections whose boxes touch the known tile-grid seam lines — the grid is deterministic, so I know exactly where every seam is — and alert on drift in that ratio. I'd also build a synthetic seam regression test into CI: inject golden objects of known count at a sweep of positions, including dead-center-on-seam, run the full tile-and-merge pipeline, and assert the recovered count matches ground truth. That runs automatically whenever the model, tile size, overlap, or merge logic changes — because a seemingly harmless throughput tweak, like shrinking tile size, can silently violate the "overlap ≥ max object size" invariant and reintroduce fragmentation without anyone noticing until counts drift downstream. On top of that, I'd run a spatial duplicate audit on live output: cluster nearby detections by center distance, flag any high-IoU pair that both survived NMS, and bucket those by whether they sit on a known seam — almost certainly a merge bug — versus genuinely dense, legitimately adjacent objects, which is an NMS-tuning question instead (same subtlety as crowd-counting NMS failures). I'd also watch for count drift against a reference baseline that correlates spatially with the tile grid — that's a strong tell for a stitching defect rather than a model accuracy problem.

**Short version:** derive patch size and overlap from the real object-size distribution rather than defaults; solve true duplicates with global NMS and fragments with deterministic core-region ownership rather than relying on IoU alone; and treat seam correctness as a versioned, continuously tested part of the pipeline — logged ratios, CI seam-injection tests, and live duplicate audits — not something checked once and assumed to hold.

#### Detailed Answer — Tiling/Patch Inference Strategy for Large-Image Tiny Object Detection

##### Designing the tiling strategy (patch size, overlap/stride, throughput vs. accuracy)

**Patch size selection**:
- Choose patch size based on the detector's **native training resolution and effective receptive field**, not arbitrary convenience — e.g., if the model was trained/validated at 640x640 or 1024x1024, tiling at that same resolution avoids introducing a train/inference scale mismatch (a tiny object that was 15px at training resolution should still be ~15px per tile at inference, not artificially shrunk by tiling at a much larger patch size then resizing down).
- Smaller patches increase the *relative* size of tiny objects within each tile (good for recall) but increase the **total number of patches** (compute cost scales roughly with image_area / patch_area), so this is a direct throughput-vs-accuracy knob, not a free win.

**Overlap/stride design**:
- Use **overlapping tiles** (stride < patch size) specifically sized to be **at least the maximum expected object size** (plus margin) — this guarantees that any object, regardless of where it falls, is **fully contained within at least one tile**, even if it's split across the boundary in adjacent tiles.
- A common practical rule: overlap = 2x the largest expected object dimension, ensuring no object can simultaneously straddle boundaries in *every* overlapping tile at once.
- Throughput cost: overlap directly increases the number of tiles (and redundant compute on the overlapping regions) — there's a real trade-off between overlap width (recall safety margin) and total inference cost, which should be tuned against your actual object-size distribution rather than using an arbitrary default (e.g., naive 50% overlap "just to be safe").

**Boundary-object stitching/merging logic**:
1. Run inference independently per tile, producing detections in **tile-local coordinates**.
2. **Transform all detections back to global image coordinates** (add the tile's offset) before any merging step — this is a common bug source if done incorrectly (e.g., forgetting to account for resize scaling between tile size and model input size).
3. Apply a **global merge step across all tiles' detections**: typically NMS (or a better alternative like Soft-NMS/DIoU-NMS, per earlier discussion) run on the **combined, global-coordinate detection set**, not independently per tile — this is what collapses the duplicate detections created when the same object is captured (fully or partially) in multiple overlapping tiles.
4. For objects **genuinely split across a tile boundary with no single tile containing the full object** (possible if your overlap margin was insufficient, or for unusually large/elongated objects): detect partial-box signatures (e.g., a predicted box touching/clipped exactly at a tile edge) and apply a **box-merging heuristic** that fuses two partial detections from adjacent tiles into one box spanning both, rather than relying on NMS alone (NMS only suppresses duplicates, it doesn't *reconstruct* a single object from two partial, non-overlapping-enough boxes).

##### Detecting and correcting duplicate/fragmented detections systematically in production (not one-off testing)

1. **Boundary-proximity flagging**: for every raw per-tile detection, record whether its box touches or lies within a small margin of that tile's edge (a simple, cheap geometric check). Route only these boundary-flagged detections through the extra stitching/merging logic, leaving interior detections untouched — this keeps the merge step cheap and targeted rather than reprocessing every detection.
2. **Systematic duplicate-rate monitoring**: track, per production batch, the **ratio of raw (pre-merge) detection count to post-merge detection count specifically in overlap regions** — a stable baseline ratio is expected due to intentional tile overlap; a significant drift (ratio spike) signals either a tiling/stitching bug (e.g., a coordinate-transform regression) or a genuine change in object density, and should be an automated alert, not something caught by chance during a demo.
3. **Synthetic boundary stress test (regression test, not one-off)**: construct a repeatable test set by taking known, fully-annotated objects and deliberately placing them (via compositing or selecting real images) at exact tile-boundary-straddling positions across the full range of possible offsets — run this as a standing regression suite on every change to the tiling/merging code, checking that these specific objects are detected exactly once with a correctly reconstructed box, not fragmented or duplicated. This directly targets the failure mode rather than relying on incidental discovery in general validation data.
4. **End-to-end count sanity check against an independent reference**: for domains where it's feasible (e.g., countable objects like vehicles/pallets), periodically compare total detected counts per image against either human-reviewed samples or a slower/more thorough reference pipeline (e.g., full overlap, finer tiling) — systematic over/under-counting trends are a strong signal of residual stitching issues that a per-tile accuracy metric alone wouldn't surface.
